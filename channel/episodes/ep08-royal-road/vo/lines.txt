A famous motto on a New York post office is actually about Persian messengers.
About two and a half thousand years ago, the Persian Empire built the Royal Road.
It ran roughly twenty-seven hundred kilometres, from Sardis to Susa.
Along the way: over a hundred stations, with fresh horses waiting.
A rider galloped one stage, then handed the message to the next rider.
Like a relay race, but with taxes and royal orders.
On foot, the whole trip took about three months.
With the relay? Reportedly, about a week!
The Greek historian Herodotus was impressed. Nothing mortal travels faster, he wrote.
Neither snow, nor rain, nor heat, nor night stops these couriers.
Centuries later, a version of that line was carved on New York's main post office.
So that delivery guy who's late again? Persia set a high bar.
Fast roads meant fast trade, fast taxes, and fast news.
Basically, the empire had express shipping.
